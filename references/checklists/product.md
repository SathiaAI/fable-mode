# Product planning, value teardowns, improvement plans

The commonest failure is fluent invention: plausible strategy unmoored from evidence. Every claim gets a label — observed (data, reviews, revenue, usage), inferred (follows from observed), or guessed (plausible, unchecked). The plan's credibility is the weakest unlabeled claim in it.

## Value teardown (existing product)

- Name the job the product is hired for, who pays, and why now. If reviews/usage data exist, quote them — that's observed; your interpretation is inferred.
- Locate where the value concentrates (the 20% of surface carrying 80% of willingness-to-pay). New features rarely fix a product whose core value is misidentified.
- Name the binding constraint before proposing anything: distribution, activation, retention, margin, or capability. A fix aimed at a non-binding constraint is wasted motion — say which constraint the evidence points to and how confident you are.
- Ask "why hasn't the incumbent/obvious competitor done this?" for each opportunity. If there's no answer, the opportunity is probably mispriced.

## Improvement / next-level plan

- Prioritize by impact × effort × confidence, shown, not asserted. Confidence comes from the evidence labels above.
- Each bet gets one measurable success metric and a kill criterion (what result means stop). Plans without kill criteria become sunk-cost machines.
- Sequence: what must be true first? Dependencies beat wishlists.
- State non-goals: what you are deliberately not improving and why.

## Verify before delivering

- Every recommendation traces to labeled evidence. Priorities survive the "why now / why us / why hasn't someone" test. Metrics and kill criteria present. SESSION.md updated.
